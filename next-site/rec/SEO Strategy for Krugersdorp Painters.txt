Decoding PMR 22 and the Reserve Fund Imperative for Exterior Maintenance
This secondary heading serves as an educational asset, breaking down dense legislation into actionable procurement data. Under the Sectional Titles Schemes Management Act, specifically Prescribed Management Rule 22 (PMR 22), every community scheme is legally required to prepare a written Maintenance, Repair, and Replacement Plan (MRRP) for major capital items spanning the next ten years.2
The content explicitly categorizes exterior painting and comprehensive waterproofing as undeniable "major capital items" under the legislation.4 The narrative explains the historical context: prior to this legislation, the majority of schemes in South Africa did not possess a reserve fund, relying instead on the standard administration fund or resorting to the ill-famed 'special levies' when facades failed or roofs leaked.4 Under the current regime, Section 3 (read with Regulation 2) and PMR 24(2) dictate that the reserve fund must be utilized specifically for the implementation of the MRRP.4 The copy emphasizes the painful reality that a 10-Year Maintenance Plan is frequently treated as a mere "tick box exercise" by failing bodies corporate.2 To transform this document into a meaningful financial instrument, trustees require exact, plain-language scopes of work that accurately project the cost of thermal shock repair, spalling remediation, and elastomeric applications over the decade, mapping cash flow with absolute precision.2
The Financial and Structural Superiority of "Phased Chunking"
This section forms the core conversion mechanism of the landing page. Trustees are routinely paralyzed by the logistical and financial nightmare of attempting to scaffold and paint an entire massive complex simultaneously.
Eradicating Capital Expenditure Spikes Traditional procurement demands a massive, single-payment capital outlay, which instantly drains the reserve fund and often forces the implementation of a special levy.4 The proposed "phased chunking" methodology systematically breaks the estate down into manageable blocks or operational precincts. By painting the complex block-by-block over a continuous 36-to-48-month cycle, the financial burden is spread evenly across multiple fiscal periods. This approach perfectly matches the biological accumulation rate of the statutory reserve fund, allowing the administration to pay for manageable, sequential phases directly from the monthly accruals rather than depleting the capital base in a single traumatic event.2
Zero Resident Friction Through Agile Delivery Large-scale contractor presences inevitably cause immense resident friction, resulting in blocked visitor parking, sustained noise pollution, and significant security vulnerabilities. By utilizing an agile, small-team delivery model focused strictly on exterior applications, the phased chunking methodology isolates the operational footprint to a highly restricted micro-zone. The agile team moves systematically, completing a cluster of units entirely before advancing to the next. This methodology guarantees zero disruption to the broader estate ecosystem and drastically simplifies the management of biometric access control for the estate security manager.18
Prolonged Asset Lifecycle Management Structurally, phased chunking allows the agile team to address micro-climatic damage progressively and intelligently. For example, north-facing facades in the West Rand experience dramatically more severe UV degradation and thermal shock, while south-facing boundary walls often suffer from persistent lateral damp and efflorescence due to poor sun exposure and soil proximity.13 Phased delivery allows for the targeted application of high-build coatings like Plascon Micatex—leveraging its specific formulation of mica and mineral quartz—precisely when and where a specific facade reaches its end-of-life, rather than waiting for the entire complex to degrade uniformly.10
Navigating the Median Line and Common Property Jurisprudence
Property disputes within sectional titles and HOAs frequently arise over the exact maintenance responsibilities regarding boundary walls, doors, and windows.23 This section establishes the contractor's unassailable legal authority on the matter.
In terms of Section 3(1)(l) of the STSM Act, the body corporate must maintain all common property.24 For boundary walls, doors, and windows set into an exterior section wall, Section 5 of the Sectional Titles Act dictates the application of the "median line" rule.24 The median line is a non-physical boundary that bisects the center of the wall. Consequently, the inner 50% is the sole responsibility of the unit owner, while the outer 50% (the exterior facade and common property-facing boundary walls) remains the strict, undivided responsibility of the body corporate.24
The copy must decisively state: "Our exterior-only teams specialize exclusively in restoring the outer 50% of the median line. We remove the burden from the body corporate by rapidly repairing spalling, treating lateral damp with deep DPC injections and PRO-STRUCT cementitious tanking, and applying durable architectural coatings to the common property facades. This entirely insulates the trustees from resident maintenance disputes and ensures uniform aesthetic compliance across the scheme." 13
