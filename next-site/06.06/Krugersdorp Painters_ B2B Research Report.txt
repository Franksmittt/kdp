The Sectional Titles Schemes Management Act (STSMA) and Prescribed Management Rule 22
The introduction of the Sectional Titles Schemes Management Act (STSMA) and its accompanying Prescribed Management Rules (PMRs) in October 2016 fundamentally reshaped the administrative and financial landscape of South African community schemes.1 Prior to the enactment of this legislation, a significant majority of community schemes in South Africa operated without dedicated reserve funds, relying instead on ad hoc financial management.2 When major exterior maintenance projects became unavoidable, the default mechanism for funding these capital expenditures was the implementation of the highly unpopular and often financially devastating "special levy".2 This reactive approach frequently led to deferred maintenance, accelerating the deterioration of building substrates and severely compromising the structural integrity of complex facades.2
Under the current legislative regime, specifically Prescribed Management Rule 22 (PMR 22), it is a non-negotiable fiduciary obligation for every body corporate to prepare a detailed, written 10-Year Maintenance, Repair, and Replacement Plan (MRRP) for all major capital items situated on the common property.4 The MRRP is not a superficial schedule; it must explicitly document the current condition of all exterior assets, outline the anticipated timelines for their repair or replacement, detail the estimated costs of these interventions, and state the anticipated life expectancy of the substrates once they have been maintained.5
This legislative mandate is financially supported through the compulsory establishment of a Reserve Fund, governed by PMR 24(2), which strictly isolates capital maintenance accruals from standard day-to-day administrative expenses.2 The legislation dictates that interest earned on these funds must be apportioned fairly between the administrative and reserve accounts, ensuring that reserve monies remain readily accessible solely for their respective capital preservation purposes.2 The annual financial contribution required from the body corporate to satisfy the obligations of the MRRP is calculated using a strict statutory formula outlined in the PMRs 5:

	Section 5 of the Sectional Titles Act and the Median Line Principle
One of the most complex operational and financial hurdles in sectional title exterior maintenance involves the allocation of responsibility for fenestration, doors, and boundary structures. Section 5 of the Sectional Titles Act (Act 95 of 1986), specifically subsections 5(4) and 5(5), dictates the boundaries between individual sections and the common property through the legal concept of the "median line".7
Historically, fierce disputes arose over whether a specific window frame or door installation fell inside or outside the structural median line, creating immense administrative friction for managing agents.7 However, following a crucial 2010 amendment drafted to resolve these recurring difficulties, the legislation now explicitly states that any window, door, or other structure dividing a section from another section or from the common property is considered to form part of that dividing floor, wall, or ceiling.7 Consequently, the median line is an imaginary boundary running directly through the geographical center of the window or door mechanism itself, regardless of where the unit is situated within the wall.6
The legal and financial effect of this provision is absolute: the inner 50% of the exterior window or door forms part of the owner's private section, while the outer 50% forms part of the scheme's common property.6 The explicit intent of this legislative provision is to compel the individual owner and the body corporate to share the maintenance, repair, and replacement costs equally on a strict 50/50 basis.9 Because modern windows and doors are complex mechanisms that must operate as a cohesive whole—where individual parts such as handles, rubber seals, and specialized hinges are rarely located in the exact physical center of the installation—the costs of maintaining all parts of the mechanism against normal wear and tear must be shared equally.10
Furthermore, it is critical to navigate the nuances introduced by the Community Schemes Ombud Service (CSOS) Circular No. 1 of 2021 regarding Exclusive Use Areas (EUAs). While the body corporate is generally responsible for performing the physical maintenance on areas subject to exclusive use rights, the legislation mandates that the body corporate must recover these specific costs from the owner who holds those rights.10 CSOS Circular No. 1 of 2021 overrides broad interpretations of the STSMA by stating that when an EUA solely services the unit of an owner, that specific owner is directly responsible for the cost of all repairs and maintenance of that area, including flower beds, lapas, and localized structural elements.10

Geological Topology and Expansive Soil Mechanics
Krugersdorp's sloped and terraced residential nodes, particularly the affluent areas of Chancliff Ridge, Rangeview, and Noordheuwel, rest upon highly complex and reactive geological formations.18 The broader regional stratigraphy, drawing structural analogues from surrounding formations, is characterized by prominent quartzite ridges, interbedded shales, and critically, significant deposits of expansive or swelling clays.22
Expansive soils represent one of the most severe natural hazards to engineering structures and shallow foundations.22 These soils are highly susceptible to drastic volume changes dictated by moisture content; as the swelling clays absorb ground water during the Highveld's intense summer thunderstorms, they expand rapidly.22 Upon expansion, the soil exerts immense upward and lateral hydrostatic pressures on structural foundations, boundary walls, and retaining structures.22 If this swelling pressure exceeds the downward foundation pressure of the townhouse or complex boundary wall, differential uplift occurs, inevitably causing walls to shear, beams to crack, and rigid exterior substrates to fracture.22
In the terraced townhouse clusters common to Chancliff Ridge and Rangeview, earth-retaining boundary walls and sloped residential foundations are subjected to relentless hydrostatic forcing during the wet season. This environmental reality requires the contractor to draw a strict, scientifically accurate distinction between traditional "rising damp" and the more destructive "lateral damp migration."
Hydrostatic Lateral Damp vs. Capillary Rising Damp
* Rising Damp (Capillary Action): This phenomenon occurs when ground moisture travels vertically up through permeable masonry and mortar joints via capillary action. It is typically characterized by a horizontal tide mark of efflorescence (salt deposits) and blistering paint, generally affecting only the lowest meter of the exterior wall.20 It is frequently the result of a failed, bridged, or completely absent physical damp-proof course (DPC) in older structures.20
* Lateral Damp Migration (Hydrostatic Pressure): This is a vastly more destructive vector, driven by the physical weight of retained wet soil pressing against subterranean or earth-retaining walls on sloped sectional titles. The hydrostatic pressure physically forces moisture, along with dissolved ground salts and minerals, horizontally through the brickwork and mortar joints. This continuous pressure causes the catastrophic failure of standard exterior paint films, leading to severe spalling and structural degradation that cannot be solved by standard surface waterproofing.
Highveld Thermal Shock and Diurnal UV Degradation
Compounding the geological challenges, the meteorological profile of Krugersdorp is defined by extreme diurnal temperature shifts and intense Highveld ultraviolet (UV) radiation. Climatic data indicates that temperatures in the region can plummet to an average low of 35°F (1.6°C) during the dry winter month of July, while soaring to average highs of 78°F (25°C) and peak temperatures exceeding 85°F (29°C) during the summer.25 Daily temperature swings frequently exceed 20°C within a rapid timeframe.25
This rapid, cyclical heating and cooling induces a phenomenon known as "Highveld thermal shock." The extreme temperature differentials force the exterior masonry, plaster, and underlying brickwork to expand and contract violently on a daily basis. Standard decorative acrylic paints lack the essential elastomeric properties required to survive this continuous kinetic movement. Consequently, the rigid paint films suffer from micro-cracking, which subsequently acts as a conduit for bulk water ingress during the rainy season, triggering a destructive cycle of damp, blistering, and substrate failure. Furthermore, the region's high altitude (averaging 1533 ft) subjects exterior coatings to intense, prolonged UV radiation, which rapidly degrades the molecular bonds of inferior polymers, leading to premature chalking and color fading.26

Chemical DPC Injection Protocols for Capillary Rising Damp
Where older sectional title complexes in Krugersdorp suffer from failed physical damp-proof courses, resulting in capillary rising damp, a chemical DPC injection protocol must be instituted.20 This process utilizes a multi-component, low-molecular-weight silane or siloxane hybrid cream (such as Microsilan or DPC Inject).28
The remediation protocol must be executed with strict adherence to the following technical steps:
1. Pre-treatment Diagnostics: The firm must utilize moisture profiling equipment and thermal imaging to definitively diagnose capillary action and rule out airborne condensation or lateral ingress.20
2. Plaster Remediation: All contaminated exterior plaster must be completely hacked off to expose the underlying bare brickwork. Crucially, the plaster must be removed to a minimum height of 1 meter, or at least 300mm above the highest visible line of salt contamination, creating a necessary buffer zone.20
3. Injection Phase: A uniform drill pattern is mapped and executed into the lowest viable mortar course (typically 1-2 brick courses above floor level).29 The aqueous silane cream, which is formulated without organic solvents and is non-flammable, is injected into the drill holes under controlled pressure using specialized dispensing equipment.28
4. Curing and Rendering: Because of its low-molecular-weight formulation, the silane migrates deeply through the mortar joints. Upon curing, it forms a permanent, water-repellent, breathable horizontal barrier.28 The wall must then be replastered exclusively using a waterproof, salt-retardant cementitious render.20 If standard plaster is used, residual masonry salts will continue to draw atmospheric moisture, causing the damp to reappear and the DIY or non-specialized effort to fail completely.20
Polymer-Modified Cementitious Tanking for Lateral Damp

The explicit technical solution is the application of a two-component, polymer-modified cementitious coating, such as PRO-STRUCT 506 Flexicoat.32

	The Phased Block-by-Block Chunking Execution Model and Direct-Response Frameworks
Large-scale exterior painting and remediation projects in sprawling sectional title complexes often descend into logistical and financial chaos. Legacy generalist contractors frequently attempt to scaffold and prep entire complexes simultaneously. This archaic approach creates weeks of restricted visitor parking, widespread dust and noise pollution, and heightened security vulnerabilities due to sprawling, unmanageable work zones. Furthermore, it demands massive, singular financial deposits that place immense strain on the body corporate's cash flow.
To fundamentally disrupt this legacy approach, the firm must introduce and champion the "Phased Block-by-Block Chunking Execution Model." This highly disciplined methodology systematically isolates the complex into sequential micro-zones (e.g., executing Phase 1 entirely on Block A before migrating to Block B). All high-pressure washing, damp remediation, substrate repair, and precise Micatex coating applications are completed within one specific block before the operational footprint shifts.
